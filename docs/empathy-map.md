# Floriograph — Empathy Map (Hypothesis)

> This map extends the original single-persona hypothesis ("The Flower Gifter" — romantic partner) to cover the full relationship scope identified for Floriograph: romantic partner, family, friend, and suitor. Each persona follows the same **Says / Thinks / Does / Feels / Pain Points / Gains Sought** structure so responses can be compared side by side as survey data comes in.

## Persona 1: The Romantic Gifter

**Status:** Best-supported persona  
**Context:** Partner in a relationship who gives/receives flowers with some regularity.

### Says

- "I didn't know what to get, so I just got roses."
- "I wanted flowers that meant something, not just looked nice."
- "I looked it up but the sites all said different things."

### Thinks

- "Am I overthinking this?"
- "Does she/he even know what these flowers mean, or does it not matter?"
- "I want this gift to say something I'm bad at saying out loud."

### Does

- Defaults to safe/generic choices (roses, "whatever's pretty") under time pressure.
- Occasionally Googles flower meanings, gets inconsistent answers, gives up, and picks by aesthetics.
- Talks to a florist for suggestions rather than researching alone.

### Feels

- Pressure to get the gift "right," especially for emotionally loaded occasions (apology, grief, anniversary).
- Mild anxiety about symbolic missteps (accidentally giving something with a bad connotation).
- Satisfaction/pride when a gift feels personally meaningful rather than generic.

### Pain Points

- No easy way to translate a specific, nuanced feeling into a concrete flower choice.
- Existing "language of flowers" references are single-word lookups, not sentence-aware.
- Time pressure (buying last-minute) works against thoughtful selection.

### Gains Sought

- Confidence that the gift reflects something true and specific.
- A shortcut from "how I feel" to "what to buy" without needing floriography expertise.
- A story/meaning they can share with the recipient ("this means X, which is why I chose it").

### Survey Signal (n=7 Romantic-Partner Respondents)

Strongest support of any persona.

- 4 of 7 cited meaning/symbolism or recipient preference as what matters most when choosing.
- Several described leaning on personal knowledge of the partner (favorite color, shared references) or pairing flowers with a written note to carry nuance a single flower cannot.
- This directly supports the "gift should say something I'm bad at saying out loud" hypothesis and the multi-flower/multi-emotion approach.
- Two respondents specifically flagged "courtship or expression of romantic interest" as an occasion — a proxy signal for the Suitor persona below, though not a clean substitute for direct suitor data.

## Persona 2: The Family Gifter

**Status:** Thin / largely hypothesis  
**Context:** Giving flowers to a parent, sibling, or other family member — often for a recurring or ritual occasion (Mother's Day, birthday, hospital visit, funeral/condolence).

### Says

- "I just get what Mom likes — she doesn't care about 'meaning.'"
- "It's for a funeral, I don't want to get this wrong."
- "It's the same flowers every year, it feels a little thoughtless."

### Thinks

- "Is there something more personal I could give than the usual bouquet?"
- "What's actually appropriate here — this isn't romantic, but it should still feel warm."
- "I don't want to seem like I didn't put thought into it just because it's family."

### Does

- Repeats the same "safe" choice year after year (whatever the family has always used).
- For sensitive occasions (illness, sympathy), relies on florist or funeral home defaults rather than personal choice.
- Rarely researches flower meaning for family gifts — treats it as lower-stakes than romantic gifting.

### Feels

- Less symbolic pressure than romantic gifting, but occasion-specific pressure spikes sharply (grief, illness) can create real anxiety about "getting it wrong."
- Guilt or flatness about repeating the same generic gift.
- Warmth/connection when a gift acknowledges something specific about the relationship (e.g., an inside reference, a shared memory).

### Pain Points

- Floriography guidance skews romantic in tone/marketing, so family-appropriate meanings are hard to find.
- Sensitive occasions (sympathy, illness) carry real risk of a culturally or symbolically wrong choice, with little easy guidance.
- Little incentive to move past "default" choices without an easy way to personalize.

### Gains Sought

- A way to make a familiar, low-stakes gift feel more personal without much extra effort.
- Reassurance/guardrails for sensitive occasions (sympathy, illness) so they don't pick something with an unintended meaning.
- Language to explain the gift's meaning that fits a family (not romantic) tone.

### Survey Signal (n=1 Family Respondent)

Thin.

- One respondent described a celebration/achievement occasion (graduation/promotion-type), chose based on recipient's favorite, and described the experience simply as feeling "loved."
- Does not contradict the hypothesis, but does not test the sympathy/illness or "repeating the same default gift" pain points either — those remain unvalidated.

## Persona 3: The Friend Gifter

**Status:** Thin / largely hypothesis  
**Context:** Giving flowers to a friend — celebration, comfort, or "just because."

### Says

- "Flowers for a friend feels like a big gesture — is that weird?"
- "I wanted to cheer her up but didn't know what would actually say that."
- "I'd never really thought about giving a friend flowers until [occasion] came up."

### Thinks

- "Will this read as more than platonic if I'm not careful?"
- "What would actually communicate 'I'm proud of you' or 'I'm here for you' through flowers?"
- "I don't know the 'rules' here the way I might for a partner or family member."

### Does

- Gifts flowers for friends less frequently and more occasion-driven (new job, breakup, achievement, tough time) than for partners/family.
- Often unsure whether flowers are even the "right" gift for a friendship; hesitates or picks something generic to stay safe.
- May ask the friend's other friends or check social media for hints rather than relying on floriography.

### Feels

- Uncertainty about tone — wants warmth without romantic implication.
- Some social self-consciousness ("is this an appropriate gift between friends?").
- Genuine desire to mark the moment meaningfully, especially for support/comfort occasions.

### Pain Points

- Almost no floriography guidance is written for platonic/friend contexts specifically — most references assume romance or generic "sympathy/celebration" categories.
- Risk of a flower choice unintentionally reading as romantic.
- Occasion-driven, so timing pressure is common (needs a fast, confident answer).

### Gains Sought

- Clear, tone-appropriate flower choices that read as platonic support/celebration, not romance.
- Speed/confidence for occasion-driven gifting (they don't want to research — they want an answer).
- A way to make a less-common gesture (flowers between friends) feel intentional rather than random.

### Survey Signal (n=1 Friend Respondent)

One rich narrative response.

- Gave flowers to multiple friends on Valentine's Day specifically to express appreciation.
- Chose flowers based on each friend's individual preferences and their symbolism.
- Described wanting to capture the feeling in one word ("cherish").
- Also handles complex/mixed emotion by pairing flowers with a written letter every time — the strongest single piece of evidence in the dataset for the "flowers alone aren't enough to carry nuance" pain point.
- Does not test the "could this read as romantic" self-consciousness hypothesis, which remains unvalidated.

## Persona 4: The Suitor

**Status:** Untested / hypothesis  
**Context:** Early-stage romantic interest — flowers given before a relationship is established (first date, expressing interest, courting).

### Says

- "I don't want to come on too strong, but I want them to notice."
- "What flower says 'I'm interested' without being over the top?"
- "I don't really know them well yet, so I don't know what they'd even like."

### Thinks

- "Is this the right level of intensity for where we are?"
- "Could this be misread — too forward, or not clear enough?"
- "I want this to stand out from what everyone else would send."

### Does

- Leans heavily on generic "romantic" signals (red roses) by default, out of uncertainty rather than genuine choice.
- Second-guesses or delays the gesture due to fear of misreading the situation.
- May ask friends for opinions on whether a flower gift is "too much" at this stage.

### Feels

- High uncertainty/vulnerability — more social risk than an established-relationship gift.
- Desire to seem thoughtful and distinctive, not generic or presumptuous.
- Excitement mixed with anxiety about the gesture landing well.

### Pain Points

- Needs a calibrated signal (interest without presumption) that generic "romantic flowers" guidance does not address — it is binary (romantic vs. not), not gradient.
- Little personal history with the recipient to draw on for personalization.
- Highest social risk of the four personas for a "wrong" read.

### Gains Sought

- A way to signal interest at the right intensity for an early-stage relationship, not just "romantic vs. not."
- Distinctiveness — something that does not feel like the default "trying too hard" bouquet.
- Confidence to act rather than delaying out of uncertainty.

### Survey Signal (n=0 Direct)

No respondent selected a suitor/pre-relationship reference relationship.

- 2 of 7 romantic-partner respondents flagged "courtship or expression of romantic interest" as an occasion within an established relationship — not quite the same as pre-relationship courting.
- This persona remains untested by direct data.
- Worth targeting specifically in future survey outreach, e.g., an explicit "someone you're interested in but not dating" relationship option if it is not offered already.

## New Signal Not Covered: The Stranger / Low-Stakes Gifter

One respondent's reference relationship was a stranger — flowers given as "a random act of kindness," with no research into meaning and a stated purpose of "showing appreciation through action."

- Does not fit any of the four personas above (all assume an ongoing or forming relationship).
- May represent a fifth, lower-stakes use case: spontaneous/anonymous generosity rather than relationship-specific communication.
- Flagged as a possible future persona rather than added formally yet — one data point is not enough to build on.

## Cross-Persona Observations

| Observation | Status |
|---|---|
| Stakes vary by occasion, not just relationship type — sympathy/family and early-suitor situations may carry similarly high perceived risk despite being different relationship categories. | Still unvalidated — no sympathy/grief or suitor respondents yet. |
| Existing floriography guidance is romance-coded by default, which underserves family, friend, and suitor use cases even though those may represent significant use of the product. | Indirectly supported — romantic partner dominates the response sample too, which mirrors this bias. |
| Speed matters more for Friend/Family gifting; calibration matters more for Suitor gifting; personal-narrative depth matters most for Romantic Partner gifting. | Partially supported: the friend and romantic respondents both showed strong personal-narrative depth — pairing flowers with letters/personal knowledge — which weakens the "speed over depth" assumption for Friend gifting specifically. | Meaning/symbolism and recipient preference outrank appearance and price as decision factors across the sample as a whole (not persona-specific). | May be more universal than relationship-dependent; worth testing directly per persona in the next round. |

## Sample Caveats

- **n=10 overall**, with only 1 respondent each for family and friend, and 0 for suitor — three of four personas are still effectively untested by direct data.
- **Skewed toward 18–24 age range** (6/10) and **female respondents** (7/10).
- Recommendation: prioritize family, friend, and suitor responses in future outreach before treating those three personas as validated.
